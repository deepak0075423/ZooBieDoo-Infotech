Forecasting and decision analytics

Forecasts that planners actually use, because the system explains itself, shows its uncertainty and records the reasons for overrides.

The problem this solves

A common pattern: an organisation invests in a forecasting model, the model performs acceptably in backtests, and the planners override it most of the time. Accuracy is then blamed, and more accuracy is bought, and the override rate does not move.

The binding constraint is usually not accuracy. It is that the forecast arrives as a bare number with no explanation, no confidence range and no way to record why a human disagreed.

How we approach it

We treat the decision as the product, not the prediction. That means interviewing the planners, reviewing a season of override decisions against outcomes, and finding where human judgement genuinely beats the model — typically new lines and promotional periods, where the model has thin signal.

The system is then built to say so: explicit confidence intervals, drivers surfaced in plain language, an explicit low-confidence state that asks for human judgement rather than guessing, and structured capture of every override.

Those overrides feed the next training cycle, and a periodic scorecard reports where the model beat the planner and where the planner beat the model. Both happen, and saying so openly is what builds trust.

What we build

A forecasting stack combining established statistical methods and gradient-boosted models for stable series with similarity-based approaches for new items, reconciled across the levels the business plans at.

A planning workspace where the forecast is one input among several, with comparable historical analogues shown alongside and every override recorded with a reason.

Retraining on a schedule tied to measured degradation rather than the calendar, with drift monitoring and alerting.

Technology

Python with scikit-learn, statsmodels or gradient-boosting libraries, orchestrated pipelines, a warehouse such as PostgreSQL, BigQuery or Snowflake, and a React planning interface.

Model performance and unit cost are tracked together, so an accuracy gain that triples inference cost is visible rather than hidden.
