The design system audit we run before writing a single component

Most design systems fail for organisational reasons, not design ones. Here is the two-week assessment that predicts which ones will survive.

SL Sarah Lindqvist
Head of Design 30 June 2026 · 8 min read

Why design systems get abandoned

A design system is a product with users, and like any product it fails when it is harder to use than the alternative. The alternative is always available: a designer can draw a new button in ninety seconds, and an engineer can write one in five minutes.

So the question that predicts survival is not "is the system well designed". It is "is the system the path of least resistance". Every audit we run is organised around that question.

Start with an honest inventory

Before proposing anything, we count what exists. In a typical mid-size product estate we find between 20 and 60 distinct button treatments, a dozen input styles and four or five type scales in active production use.

The count is not for shaming anyone. It is for two specific purposes: it establishes the scale of the consolidation, and it reveals why the variants exist. A surprising proportion turn out to be legitimate — a genuine need the existing system did not serve — and those become requirements rather than violations.

We tag every variant as duplicate, legitimate variant, or legacy, and the ratio tells us whether we are looking at a governance problem or a coverage problem. They need very different remedies.

Governance is the hard part

The questions that predict a system's survival at eighteen months are almost entirely organisational:

  • Who is allowed to add a component, and what is the process? If the answer is "raise a ticket with a team that has other priorities", teams will fork instead.
  • Who maintains it, and is that time protected? A system maintained in slack capacity decays at exactly the rate the roadmap gets busy.
  • How does a breaking change reach consumers? Versioning, changelog and migration guidance need to exist before the first breaking change, not after.
  • What happens when a team needs something the system does not have — this week?

That last question is the one most systems answer badly. The honest answer is an escape hatch: a documented way to build something outside the system, clearly marked, with a route back in. Systems without an escape hatch do not prevent divergence; they just make it invisible.

Tokens before components

The single highest-leverage artefact is the token layer, and it is worth getting right before any component work starts. Colour, spacing, type, radius, elevation and motion, defined once and referenced everywhere.

Two practical rules from repeated experience. First, name tokens by role rather than by value — surface-raised rather than grey-100 — or the first theme change will require renaming everything. Second, keep the primitive layer small: a scale of ten spacing values that people actually use beats a scale of thirty that they round off.

With tokens in place, a theme change or a brand refresh becomes a configuration change. Without them, it becomes a project.

Measure adoption, not completeness

Teams building design systems report on component count. It is the wrong metric, because it measures supply rather than demand.

What to measure instead: the proportion of production screens built from system components, the number of one-off components created per sprint, and the median time from a team needing something to having it. Those three numbers tell you whether the system is winning.

A system with 30 components at 85 percent adoption is healthy. A system with 200 components at 30 percent adoption is a museum.
